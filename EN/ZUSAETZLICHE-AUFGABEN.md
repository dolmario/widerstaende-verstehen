# Ten guided resistor exercises with worked reasoning

All numbers are calculated predictions or explicitly fictional diagnostic examples. No self-performed measurements. New MOSS films remain in preparation. Proposed activities require supervision and review of actual equipment.

## 1. Your workspace and record

First open the companion download from the description and extract it into your own folder. The English folder contains worksheets, solutions and our circuit diagrams. Read the proposed activity instructions before gathering equipment. Choose just the single resistor at five volts for your first task. On a blank sheet make three headings: prediction, observation and explanation. Put the component value and calculated current under prediction. Leave observation blank until you have actually measured something. Explanation will later contain your reason for a difference. You can complete this preparation without connecting a supply. Mark both resistor terminals on the circuit diagram. Ask a second person to check that you understand the same measurement points. Measuring at different positions can mean comparing different quantities. Label every point and write the unit explicitly. Keep the original worksheet and your completed copy under different filenames.

## 2. Calculation: 2.2 kilohms at five volts

Try a new value before hearing the solution: 2.2 kilohms at five volts. What current does the ideal model predict? Write two thousand two hundred ohms first. Divide five volts by two thousand two hundred ohms. The result is approximately zero point zero zero two two seven three amperes. Multiply by one thousand to express it in milliamps. You obtain approximately 2.273 milliamps. Check the direction of the change: the resistance is greater than one kilohm, so the current at the same voltage must be below five milliamps. For a reverse check multiply the unrounded current by the resistance. You recover five volts. Round only at the end and mark the result as approximate. Finally record the assumptions: a constant nominal resistance and five volts actually across that component. A supply setting alone would not be a measurement of the component voltage.

## 3. Check the tolerance yourself

A resistor is marked one kilohm with five percent tolerance. Find both nominal limits first. Five percent of one thousand ohms is fifty ohms. The lower limit is therefore nine hundred and fifty ohms, and the upper limit is one thousand and fifty ohms. An assumed value of nine hundred and eighty ohms lies within that interval. This is still not an actual measurement. Calculate its relative deviation: nine hundred and eighty minus one thousand is minus twenty ohms. Divide by one thousand and multiply by one hundred to obtain minus two percent. At exactly five volts the predicted current would be five divided by nine hundred and eighty, approximately 5.102 milliamps. Explain why the smaller resistance produces a larger current. In a real assessment also consider measurement uncertainty and temperature. Printed tolerance is not a general error bound for the whole circuit or for your meter.

## 4. Select resistance and rating separately

You want an ideal current of ten milliamps from five volts. Divide five volts by zero point zero one amperes to obtain five hundred ohms. A common E twelve series instead offers values such as four hundred and seventy or five hundred and sixty ohms. Calculate both candidates. Four hundred and seventy ohms gives approximately 10.64 milliamps; five hundred and sixty gives approximately 8.93 milliamps. Your actual task must specify which deviation is acceptable. A preferred series value is not automatic approval. Next check power: five volts squared divided by four hundred and seventy ohms is approximately 0.0532 watts. For five hundred and sixty ohms it is approximately 0.0446 watts. A suitable rated power also depends on the datasheet and operating conditions. Record resistance selection and power assessment separately rather than installing the nearest value without checking.

## 5. Include the voltmeter in the calculation

Consider two equal one megohm resistors forming a divider at five volts. Without an attached load, the nominal voltage across the lower resistor is half the supply, or 2.5 volts. Now assume a meter input resistance of ten megohms. It appears in parallel with the lower one megohm resistor. Their parallel equivalent is approximately 0.909 megohms. Add the upper resistor to obtain approximately 1.909 megohms total. The divider then predicts approximately 2.381 volts at the meter. That is a theoretical value, not a fabricated reading. Draw the meter resistance as a third resistor. You can then see why a reading below 2.5 volts does not automatically prove a damaged component. For a real instrument read the input resistance for the selected range in its manual. A different input resistance changes the calculation. Use the specification of the actual meter.

## 6. Reverse-check the series circuit

Our series circuit uses one kilohm and 2.2 kilohms at five volts. This time start with the result and check backwards. Nominal total current is 1.5625 milliamps. Multiply that current in amperes by one thousand ohms. You obtain 1.5625 volts across the first resistor. Multiply the same current by two thousand two hundred ohms to obtain 3.4375 volts across the second. Adding both voltages gives exactly five volts. Mark a separate voltage bracket across each resistor but a single shared current arrow along the only path. Next check power. The first resistor dissipates approximately 2.441 milliwatts; the second approximately 5.371 milliwatts. Together they dissipate 7.8125 milliwatts. Supply voltage multiplied by total current gives the same total. This independent check can reveal a calculation or unit error before any practical circuit is energised.

## 7. Work through the loaded divider

Draw a five volt divider with two one kilohm resistors. Add a one kilohm load in parallel with the lower resistor. Which pair can you combine first? The lower resistor and the load share the same two nodes. Two equal one kilohm resistors in parallel give five hundred ohms. Only then add the upper one kilohm in series to obtain fifteen hundred ohms total. Current through the upper resistor is approximately 3.333 milliamps. Output voltage is that current multiplied by five hundred ohms, approximately 1.667 volts. Both lower branches have the same output voltage, so each carries approximately 1.667 milliamps. Adding the branch currents recovers the current through the upper resistor. If you added all three resistance values, you read the connections incorrectly. Circle the two shared nodes first. Electrical connections matter more than where symbols sit on the page.

## 8. Check a simple parallel circuit

For this new exercise use just two equal one kilohm resistors in parallel across five volts. Predict whether total resistance is above or below one kilohm. Two current paths mean a lower equivalent resistance. Each branch carries five milliamps, so the supply delivers ten milliamps total. Five volts divided by zero point zero one amperes gives five hundred ohms equivalent resistance. Conductance confirms it: one divided by one thousand plus one divided by one thousand equals zero point zero zero two siemens. Its reciprocal is five hundred ohms. Each resistor dissipates five volts squared divided by one thousand ohms, or twenty-five milliwatts. Total dissipation is fifty milliwatts. Supply voltage multiplied by total current also gives zero point zero five watts. Record voltage, branch currents and total current separately. This prevents confusing the current through one component with current delivered to the entire circuit.

## 9. Double voltage, quadruple power

A one hundred ohm resistor is initially considered at three volts. Predicted current is thirty milliamps and power is zero point zero nine watts. What changes at six volts if resistance stays constant in the simplified model? Current doubles to sixty milliamps, but power becomes six volts squared divided by one hundred ohms, or zero point three six watts. Power increases by a factor of four. A quarter watt resistor would already exceed its nominal rating under that calculated continuous load. This is a decision exercise, not an instruction to deliberately overload a component. For real selection check the datasheet, ambient temperature, mounting and margin. You cannot calculate a particular surface temperature from zero point three six watts without thermal boundary conditions. Do not use touching an energised or recently switched off component as a temperature test. Document the power calculation and the allowed operating conditions. Practical approval belongs to the supervisor of the actual setup.

## 10. Investigate a difference methodically

Finish with a fictional diagnostic case. You expect five milliamps from one kilohm at five volts, but someone writes zero milliamps in a log. Name three possible causes without presenting any as observed. A broken current path, an unsuitable meter connection or an inactive output could produce such a result. A wrong range or an instrument problem are further possibilities. First check which quantity was meant to be measured and whether the measurement plan fits. Then compare wiring against the circuit diagram with the output switched off. Only an approved check on the actual equipment yields new observations. Keep hypotheses, verified findings and conclusions separate in the report. Once a cause is identified, change one thing and repeat the comparison under documented conditions. Do not finish by writing only works. Record component values, measurement points, actual results and your reasoned explanation. That makes the worksheet understandable to another person and useful for your next experiment.
